03 / A PRACTICAL START

Learn less.
Use it more.

A focused routine for your first month. A fast start, not a promise of fluency.

Your daily 25 minutes

5 min: recall yesterday's cards. 10 min: learn 10–15 new words. 5 min: listen and repeat short German sentences. 5 min: describe your day aloud. Review before adding more.

WEEK 01

Build your survival kit.

Study the first 100 words. Practice introductions, polite requests, numbers, and questions. Learn whole chunks, not isolated translations.

Hallo! Ich heiße ...
Hello! My name is ...

Ich lerne Deutsch. Bitte langsam.
I'm learning German. Slowly, please.

Was bedeutet das?
What does that mean?

WEEK 02

Make simple sentences.

Add common verbs and nouns. In a main statement, the conjugated verb is the second element (not necessarily the second word).

Ich trinke heute Wasser.
I am drinking water today.

Heute trinke ich Wasser.
Today I am drinking water.

German nouns start with capitals. Learn das Wasser (water), not just Wasser.

WEEK 03

Connect your thoughts.

Add time words, adjectives, and connectors. Use und (and), aber (but), and weil (because). With weil, the conjugated verb normally goes last in its clause.

Ich lerne, weil ich in Berlin arbeiten möchte.
I'm studying because I'd like to work in Berlin.

Write three true sentences about your own life every day.

WEEK 04

Turn recall into conversation.

Role-play a café order, directions, a shop visit, and a short introduction. Retell a short audio clip in your own words. Keep daily reviews and revisit words you hesitate on.

Ich hätte gern einen Kaffee.
I'd like a coffee.

Können Sie das bitte wiederholen?
Could you repeat that, please?

Articles: tiny words, important work.

der, die, and das all mean "the." Gender is grammatical, not reliably predictable from meaning. Case changes the article. The CSV's noun article is nominative singular, not the article for every sentence.

Definite articles by case

Genitive / possessiondesderdesder

A/an: nominative ein Mann, eine Frau, ein Kind; accusative einen Mann, eine Frau, ein Kind. There is no plural indefinite article. Dative plural nouns usually add -n unless already ending in -n or -s.

Hear the difference.

ei sounds like the vowel in "my"; ie like "see"; w like English "v"; z like "ts"; sch like "sh"; ß like "ss". For ü, say "ee" while rounding your lips; for ö, round your lips while saying "eh". These are starting cues, not substitutes for native audio.

Numbers 1–10: eins, zwei, drei, vier, fünf, sechs, sieben, acht, neun, zehn. For 21, German says "one-and-twenty": einundzwanzig.

What "5,000 common words" means here

This deck selects 5,000 distinct German words with usable English dictionary senses, ordered by their frequency in OpenSubtitles 2018. It is a corpus-based study selection, not a universal or certified top-5,000 list. Film dialogue favors conversation, drama, slang, and sometimes adult vocabulary over formal writing.

Nonwords, proper-name-only entries, unresolved inflections, and nouns without sourced articles are excluded. Study ranks therefore differ from the original corpus ranks. Counts refer to surface spellings, not combined inflections. Some inflected function words remain because they are useful to learn. Nouns are capitalized for display.

English glosses and grammar are dictionary-derived, with common function-word explanations edited for learning. Unambiguous inflected adjective/verb forms use the base word's English meaning; card notes identify the base and grammatical form, so the English is not necessarily tense-specific. One primary word family and a few senses are shown; a word can have other roles, genders, or meanings. Automated matching is not a full teacher review, and subtitle casing can make homographs ambiguous.
